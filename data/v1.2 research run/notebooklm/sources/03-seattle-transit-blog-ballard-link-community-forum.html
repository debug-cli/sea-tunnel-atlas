Ballard Link Community Forum

Posted on October 21, 2026October 21, 2026 by Martin Pagel

Dan Strauss invited Sound Transit board members, city officials, and state officials to a Ballard Link Extension (BLE) Community Forum Monday to provide the public with information on the Ballard Link extension plans. The Nordic Museum’s auditorium was packed with residents from Ballard and along the route as well as from the Eastside and even Tacoma. After short opening remarks from the officials, Sound Transit CEO Constantine introduced Brad Owen (acting Chief Delivery Officer, former Los Angeles Metro), Lauren Matthias (who joined the BLE team as Project Director from Vancouver’s Broadway SkyTrain) and Dan Turner (long time BLE Project Manager). Then Dan Strauss took questions from the audience.

While many aspects were discussed, it became clear that Sound Transit does not envision any major changes but is focusing on reducing the cost of the project as envisioned. They are hoping Olympia will enact increasing debt allowance (by borrowing for 75 years instead of 40, which effectively reduces debt burden by $1b) and a better federal funding environment (faster grants).

Brad Owen pointed out that they already reduced the West Seattle Link Extension (WSLE) cost by $2b (after the cost had quadrupled). He said that they have already identified potential savings of $1-3b for BLE (such as a smaller trapezoidal Seattle Center station). They have just concluded an industry request for information (RFI). Some suggestions (from Alstrom, contractors, and others) had already been incorporated; others will be. In order to deliver BLE by 2042, they need to shed about $10b, otherwise it may take longer. The audience voiced concerns whether more stations would get dropped (such as Avalon and South Lake Union), and asked why WSLE was prioritized over BLE when estimates for BLE ridership are four times as high. Brad Owen pointed out that projects usually get phased in (such as L.A.’s Purple Line) and WSLE was approved first.

ST has studied automated trains. As those could not interoperate with the Rainier Line, it could only work from Ballard to Westlake and would require different trains and a separate maintenance facility. They don’t think the existing tunnel can be tapped safely to reach the existing SODO maintenance center. They would lose the regional funding (49%) currently committed for the second downtown tunnel. Brad Owen said: “In order to introduce new trains, you have to isolate the line, meaning Ballard to West Seattle, not the line extension, as it’s currently planned… Even with $2b savings for station size, we would introduce less capacity to the system and need new fleet and maintenance upgrades. It won’t actually reduce cost. We have already started to reduce the station size such as in Alaska Junction station.”

They have not studied an elevated alternative as it would require that they start the project from scratch (new scoping document). Downtown is too tight for big guideways to meet NEPA requirements (such as fire code) and would require too much utility relocations and disturbance.

While transit riders wait for BLE, somebody asked for Metro/SDOT to provide some mitigation until the line becomes available. The mayor promised to look into adding more bus lanes and discuss service improvements with Metro. Dan Strauss pointed out that express lines were cancelled during Covid and are still not reestablished. It will be important to get the Seattle Transit Measure passed and then work with Metro. KCC Baron mentioned that Metro will start a major restructure next year.

Dan Strauss reaffirmed to the audience that failure is not an option.

Just look at Vancouver, our nearest neighbor. (It is so frustrating to me that I’m constantly telling our leaders: Look to Vancouver!)

      additional delay, additional process

      I call bullshit. Once again there is this ridiculous idea that everything is ready to go. It is obviously not. There hasn’t been much planning at all. It doesn’t matter if we do more. Yet as long as they suggest it is the process that is slowing us down (and not the very expensive real work at the end of it) then they can avoid changing things. It is worth noting that Toronto did this very thing. They decided very late in the game to use automated trains with smaller stations for the new Ontario Line (which used to be called the “Relief Line” as it is partially meant to handle the huge loads found in Toronto). They found that they really could get by with smaller trains and smaller stations, which leads me to the next point:

      less capacity in the system

      Again, bullshit. The Ballard Line is meant to tie into the line that runs through Rainier Valley. That line can not run that often (because it runs on the surface). In contrast there is no reason why trains going from Ballard to West Seattle — on a line being built now — can’t be designed for very frequent, automated service. It is also worth noting that our light rail trains are not particularly capacious. Not compared to a light metro (like SkyTrain). They can handle a lot less people per linear foot. The first (and obvious) reason is that you don’t have to make space for the driving compartment. Thus right off the bat it is obvious that an automated line would have more capacity. Trains half the size of a four-car Link train running every two minutes would carry more people than a a four-car Link train every four minutes. Yet we can’t run the train to Ballard every four minutes because the train runs in Rainier Valley. But it is more than that. We could run every 90 seconds (if we designed it that way).

      Furthermore, there is the design of the train cars. Look at the new train cars in New York. It is obvious you can fit a lot of people in there. Look at Vancouver’s new train cars. It can’t handle quite as many people but it still has wider aisles. Now look at ours. You can’t possibly squeeze as many people in there. There is good reason for that. I would hate to sit sideways (like on the NYC subway) on a trip from Lynnwood or Federal Way to downtown. Even the SkyTrain approach might be less than ideal (not enough front-facing seats). Our trips are long by nature. But that doesn’t describe Ballard-West Seattle. Those trips are bound to be short since the line itself will be short — shorter than all of the Canadian lines and most of those in New York. It can be designed like a SkyTrain car or even the cars in New York. Put all of these ideas together and Turner has it backwards. An automated line running from West Seattle to Ballard would have much greater capacity, even if the stations were half as big.

      Then there is the reference to the second tunnel. As many have noted, we don’t need it. Not yet. Just run an automated line from Ballard to Westlake for now. This would mean a second maintenance yard — most likely in Interbay. But by delaying the construction of a second tunnel you save an estimated four billion dollars (according to Sound Transit). This is on top of the savings that come from smaller stations (probably over a billion). Make plans to eventually extend it to West Seattle (why not). In the meantime placate those in West Seattle the way they are trying to placate those in Ballard. Improve the bus system. Things in West Seattle could improved far more easily — and with much better results — than in Ballard. (West Seattle has a bridge that doesn’t open and no stop lights at all for a bus until it gets to the edge of downtown. In contrast Ballard has a draw bridge with really bad congestion along with numerous traffic lights along the way.) But by all means, plan on running trains to West Seattle. Put Avalon back on the table. Work on improving the downtown tunnel. Not a half-ass think like the current plans but something that adds real value with a stop at First Hill. Do it right and stop pretending it is “the process” that is forcing you to screw it up.
